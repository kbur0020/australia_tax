A data investigation

The Australian
Tax Paradox

Australians paid $770 billion in federal expenses last year. The government chose not to collect another $269 billion through tax concessions — most of which flow to the wealthiest Australians.
This is the story of where the money goes, who benefits, and what it could otherwise fund.

By Kai Burke · Monash University · FIT2179 · May 2026
I How much we earn — and where

Before we ask what happens to tax money, we should look at the people who pay it. In 2022–23, more than 14 million Australians lodged a tax return. The taxable income they reported tells us a great deal about how unequally wealth is distributed across this country — and especially, across Victoria.

Stretch the lens nationally and the gap widens dramatically. The single highest-earning postcode in Australia, Sydney's Darling Point / Edgecliff (2027), reports an average taxable income of $279,712 — sixty-nine times the average in Bungunya, rural Queensland (4494). Both are filled with Australians filing the same tax return; the system that taxes them is the same. What follows from each return is not.

The top ten and bottom ten are striking extremes, but they are also outliers. To see the full shape of the country, every one of the 2,622 postcodes that lodged tax returns can be plotted on a single line — grouped by state, with each state's median marked.

Geography is one driver. Profession is another. The ten highest-earning occupations cluster almost entirely in medicine and finance — five of the top ten are surgeons, anaesthetists or other specialists.

That national top ten paints one picture. But Australia's labour market varies sharply by state. Compared side-by-side, the top earners in each state reveal where the country's economic structures differ — and where they are remarkably similar.

II Where the money goes

In the 2024–25 financial year, the federal government spent $770 billion. That is roughly $29,000 per Australian. The vast majority of this — more than three quarters — flowed into just three areas: social security and welfare, health, and inter-government transfers to the states.

The official 2024–25 budget set out estimates for each function in May 2024. Twelve months later, the Final Budget Outcome revealed how close those estimates were to reality. Some functions overshot dramatically — notably Health, which spent $15 billion more than budgeted on health services. Others fell well short: Housing delivered only $3.3 billion of the $12 billion that was promised.

These are the visible numbers — what the government collects and what it then spends. But the public ledger is only half the story. Alongside the explicit budget runs a parallel system of tax breaks, exemptions, and concessions: revenue the government has a right to collect, but chooses not to.

III The hidden cost: $269 billion forgone

Australia's Tax Expenditures and Insights Statement — published every year by Treasury — quantifies the revenue forgone through every concession, exemption, and discount in the tax code. In 2025–26 the total comes to $269 billion (net). To put that number in perspective: it is roughly twice the entire federal Health budget.

Not all concessions behave the same way over time. Some are stable; others are shrinking; a small number are growing fast enough to change the shape of the tax system in just a few years. Plotting size against projected growth makes the trajectory visible at a glance.

Defenders of the system point out that many of these concessions help middle Australia: superannuation helps people save for retirement, the main residence exemption protects the family home. But Treasury's own distributional analysis tells a more uncomfortable story. The benefits are dramatically concentrated among high-income earners.

The concentration is not only by income. Treasury's Table 2.1 shows that for every major concession, men receive a larger average benefit than women — a gap that reflects the underlying gender pay gap and lower female superannuation balances, but is then amplified by the structure of the concessions themselves.

And these costs are not stable. Treasury's projections show the three biggest concessions — super contributions, super earnings, and the CGT discount — climbing from a combined $63 billion in 2021–22 to a projected $92 billion by 2028–29. That is a 44% increase in just seven years, growing faster than nominal GDP.

It is one thing to read $269 billion as an abstract number. It is another to see what the same money builds when it is collected and spent directly. Here the tax expenditure total is laid out beside the major federal spending programs, with each square representing five billion dollars.

Australia is, by international standards, a low-to-middle taxed country: its tax-to-GDP ratio sits below the OECD average. But that statistic conceals a deeper question — not how much we pay, but who we ask to pay it, and what we choose not to collect at all.

The $269 billion in foregone revenue is not waste. Each concession was once justified, debated, and legislated. But the cumulative effect — a tax system in which the wealthiest Australians receive the largest concessions, and the gap between collected and forgone narrows each year — is a structural feature of public finance worth examining honestly.
