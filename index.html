A clear look at the numbers

The Australian
Tax Paradox

Last year the federal government spent $770 billion running the country. But it also gave away another $269 billion in tax breaks — quietly, with most of the benefit going to people who already have plenty.
Here is the story, in pictures.

By Kai Burke · Monash University · FIT2179 · May 2026
I How much we earn — and where

Before we ask what happens to our tax money, it helps to look at the people who pay it. In the 2022–23 financial year, more than 14 million Australians lodged a tax return. The amount of income they reported reveals a striking gap between where the highest earners live and where the lowest earners live.

Zoom out to the whole country and the gap gets even wider. The single highest-earning postcode in Australia, Darling Point in Sydney, reports an average income of $279,712 per person. The lowest, Bungunya in rural Queensland, reports just $4,047. That is a 69-fold difference — in the same country, under the same tax system.

These are extremes. To see the bigger picture, every postcode in Australia can be plotted on a single chart, grouped by state.

Where you live is one factor. What you do for a living is another. The country's highest-paid jobs are dominated by one profession.

That is the national picture. But each state has its own economy, and the top jobs look slightly different in each one.

II Where the money goes

In the 2024–25 financial year, the federal government spent $770 billion. That works out to around $29,000 for every person in Australia. Most of it — more than three-quarters — went into just three areas: helping people who need it (welfare and pensions), keeping people healthy (Medicare and hospitals), and sending money to the states.

Every year, the government sets a budget that says how much it plans to spend on each area. Twelve months later, we find out how close those plans were to reality. Some areas overshoot; others fall short.

Those are the numbers you can see in the budget — what the government collects and what it spends. But there is a second set of numbers that gets far less attention: the tax money the government has a right to collect, but chooses not to.

III The hidden cost: $269 billion in tax breaks

Every year the Treasury publishes a report called the Tax Expenditures and Insights Statement. It lists every tax break, exemption, and discount built into our tax system. In 2025–26, the total cost of these tax breaks is $269 billion. That is nearly twice the entire federal Health budget. Gone, every year, before the government has spent a single dollar.

Some tax breaks stay roughly the same size year after year. Others are growing fast enough to reshape the tax system within a decade. Putting both pieces of information on a single chart shows which ones to watch.

Defenders of these tax breaks say they help everyday Australians. Superannuation tax breaks help people save for retirement; the family home exemption protects the place you live in. That is true as far as it goes. But Treasury's own numbers show that the actual dollar benefit is sharply skewed toward people who already earn a lot.

The skew is not only about income. Treasury also breaks the numbers down by gender. For nearly every major tax break, men receive a bigger average benefit than women — partly because men earn more on average, and partly because the tax breaks themselves reward higher earners more.

These costs are not staying still either. Treasury's own projections show the three biggest tax breaks — the two super tax breaks and the capital gains tax discount — growing rapidly between 2021 and 2029.

It is easy to read $269 billion as just a number. It is much harder to feel. To make the scale clear, here is the same money laid out beside the actual federal spending it could replace — with each square representing $500 million in annual spending.

By world standards, Australia is not a heavily taxed country. Our overall tax take is below the average for wealthy nations. But that statistic hides a more interesting question: it is not about how much we pay, but about who we ask to pay it — and what we choose not to collect at all.

The $269 billion in tax breaks is not wasteful. Each one was justified, debated, and signed into law. But the overall effect — a tax system in which the biggest tax breaks go to the people who already earn the most, and where the gap between what we collect and what we give back keeps growing — is worth looking at honestly.
